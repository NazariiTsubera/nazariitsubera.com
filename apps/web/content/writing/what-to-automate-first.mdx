Every owner I talk to has a list. Somewhere in the week there is work that a person does by hand, again and again, and everyone knows a computer should be doing it. The list is rarely the problem. The problem is knowing where to start, because the first project sets the tone for every one after it. Pick well and automation becomes something you reach for. Pick badly and it becomes the expensive thing that did not work.

Here is the test I use.

## Three questions

A task is a good first candidate when the answer to all three is yes.

**Does it repeat?** Daily or weekly, in more or less the same shape each time. Sending invoices repeats. Preparing the one big proposal a quarter does not.

**Does it cost real hours?** Add up who does it and for how long. Ten minutes a day is an hour a week. An hour a week across three people is a working day a month. That is what the automation has to beat.

**Does it need judgement?** The best first project needs none. The rule is already written down somewhere, even if only in someone's head: when a job is marked done, send the invoice. When a card fails, send a reminder. If the person doing it has to think, stop and decide, or phone someone, it is not first.

## Where the candidates hide

The same handful of chores turn up in almost every small business:

- **Invoicing.** Creating the invoice from a finished job, sending it, chasing it.
- **Reminders.** Appointments, renewals, overdue payments, follow-ups after a quote.
- **Scheduling.** Booking, confirming, rescheduling, and the messages around each.
- **Copying between systems.** Figures that go from the till to the spreadsheet to the accounts, by hand, every week.
- **Reporting.** The numbers someone assembles every Monday from three places.

If you are not sure, watch for the word "just". "I just export it and paste it in." "I just send them a text." "Just" almost always marks a routine that a machine could own.

## What not to start with

Anything that changes every week is a bad first project, because you will be rewriting the rule as fast as you automate it. So is anything customer-facing where tone matters, at least until you have seen how the tools behave. And anything you do twice a year is not worth the setup, however annoying it is.

Leave the biggest, most tangled process for later, too. It is tempting to go after the thing that hurts most, but the first project should be the one most likely to work, not the one that would save the most if it did.

## Measure before and after

Write down the hours the task takes now. Not a guess: ask the people who do it to note it for one week. Then agree what "done" looks like for the automation: the invoice goes out within an hour of the job closing, say, and nobody touches it unless something is unusual.

Two weeks after it goes live, count again. You should be able to say, in one sentence, how many hours a month came back and what they are being spent on instead. If you cannot, the project is not finished.

## The first week

Pick one task that passes the three questions. Write the rule down in plain words. Have it built so that the exceptions, the cases the rule does not cover, land in front of a person rather than being guessed at. Then let it run and watch it for a week.

That is the whole method. The second project is easier than the first, and by the third, the list you started with looks different: not a list of chores, but a list of things you no longer do.
