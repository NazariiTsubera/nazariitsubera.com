## The moment it is built for

A vendor at a weekend market says yes. Twenty minutes later, before they have packed up, they are looking at their own website on their phone: their products, their words, a number people can tap to text them. That moment is the whole product. Everything else exists to make it reliable.

## What happens in those twenty minutes

I record a short conversation at the booth and take photos of the work. From there a pipeline takes over:

1. **Transcription and content.** The recording is transcribed and turned into structured content: name, what they make, how to order. Only facts from the conversation are used, and a guard checks the finished page against them so nothing is invented.
2. **Photos.** Product photos are normalised, backgrounds removed, and cut into responsive sizes with all metadata stripped.
3. **Authoring.** A model writes the page itself, from a design brief that fixes the rules and leaves the design open. No two vendors get the same template.
4. **The gate.** The page must pass HTML lint, the fabrication guard, and headless Chromium with accessibility checks at three widths in light and dark mode. It gets two chances to repair itself; if it still fails, a curated template renders the same content as the floor.
5. **Publishing.** The result is a single static HTML page with no JavaScript, served on the vendor's own subdomain with a seven-day preview.

## Why static and why a gate

The page has to load instantly on a cheap phone with poor signal, in the middle of a market. Static HTML with inline CSS and no scripts does that. A model authoring each page gives every vendor a design of their own, and the gate turns "the model usually gets it right" into a property the system guarantees.
